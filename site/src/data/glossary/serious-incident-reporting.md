definition: |-
  The duty falls on the provider, the company that built the system, and sometimes on the deployer, the company running it. It applies once a malfunction leads to death, serious harm, or a serious breach of someone's fundamental rights.

  Article 73 fixes the clock in days, not in vague urgency. The default is fifteen days from when the provider becomes aware of the incident. A widespread infringement or a serious disruption to critical infrastructure tightens that to two days. A death tightens it further, to ten days. Every deadline runs from awareness, not from the incident itself.

  Once the report is filed, the provider has to investigate: assess the risk, take corrective action, and cooperate with the authority. It cannot alter the system in a way that would affect the investigation's findings without telling the authority first. The duty also covers serious harm to property or the environment, not only harm to people, though that case still runs on the fifteen-day default.

## Why it comes up

It surprises companies who assume that deploying a high-risk system is a one-time compliance exercise, the same way a data-breach notification law surprises a company that has never had to report one before. A board's real question is usually simpler - if something goes badly wrong, does the company already know who calls the regulator, or would that decision be made live, under pressure, by whoever happens to be in the room.

## What to do about it

Decide in advance, in writing, who has the authority to judge that an incident is serious enough to report, rather than leaving that call to whoever notices the problem first. Preserve the system's state at the time of the incident for investigation, instead of immediately patching it away.
