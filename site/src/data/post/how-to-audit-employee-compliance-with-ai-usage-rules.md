## Where should an AI usage audit start?

How to audit employee compliance with AI usage rules: select work and compare it with the rules that applied at the time. Agree on the teams, tools, work period and decisions the audit will cover. Write down what sits outside that scope, including personal accounts or suppliers you cannot inspect.

Collect the policy and its earlier versions, the approved tool list, exception records and relevant work instructions. Check who can confirm when each version took effect. If the dates are unclear, record that gap before judging an employee against a later rule.

Choose an audit owner who can obtain evidence and a separate reviewer who can challenge disputed findings. Involve privacy, security and employee representatives where appropriate before collecting records. Tell staff what the review covers, what evidence it may use and how they can correct an error.

An [AI acceptable use policy](/blog/ai-acceptable-use-policy-for-a-50-person-company) can help identify the rules to test. If the policy leaves a decision to a manager, include the manager's decision in the evidence plan.

## How do you turn AI rules into audit tests?

Make each test specific enough that another reviewer could reach the same result. Name the rule, the work it covers, the evidence needed and the decision point. Keep the policy wording beside the test so that a reviewer can spot a mismatch.

| Rule to test                | Evidence to seek                        | Question for the reviewer                                |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Use an approved tool        | Approval list and access or work record | Was this tool approved for this use at the time?         |
| Restrict sensitive input    | Work record or limited content review   | Did the material sent fit the permitted data category?   |
| Review an output before use | Review record and released work         | Is there evidence of the required review before release? |
| Obtain an exception         | Exception decision and its scope        | Did the approval cover the actual work?                  |

Treat the table as a starting point, not a substitute for the actual policy. A rule that says employees must use judgment needs a stated test before anyone scores it. Ask the policy owner what conduct the rule requires and record the answer.

Where a rule has no practical evidence, mark it as hard to test. A missing test may call for a policy change or a better work record, rather than a finding against staff.

## Which employee work should the audit sample?

Build a list of work you can reasonably identify within the agreed scope. Combine approved tool records with work records, team interviews and reports of tools used outside the approval process. Each source has blind spots, so document what it can and cannot reveal.

Choose cases across teams, tool types and uses. Give more attention to work that handles sensitive material or affects customers, while still including routine use. Record how each case was selected and avoid letting managers choose only examples they expect to pass.

Define the population before reporting a result. For example, a sample drawn from approved company accounts says little about use through personal accounts. If access records are missing or a team cannot provide work records, describe that limit alongside the result.

Ask staff how they use tools and give them a route to report use without assuming misconduct. The site's [guidance on tools already used without approval](/faq/what-do-we-do-about-tools-staff-are-already-using-without-approval) offers a starting point for that conversation. Add newly identified work to the audit plan if it changes the risk picture.

## What evidence can show whether a rule was followed?

Start with the least detailed record that can answer the test. An access grant may show that a person could use an approved tool, while a work record may show which tool was used. Neither record alone proves that the input was permitted or that the output received review.

For a review requirement, look for an approval, tracked edit or other record tied to the released work. Check its timing and scope. A general sign-off for a project may not show that someone reviewed a particular output.

For data handling, first ask the work owner to identify the input category and explain how it was checked. If that evidence is not enough, consider a limited content review with an agreed reason and restricted access. Avoid copying full conversations into the audit file when a smaller excerpt will answer the question.

Where the GDPR applies, personal data must be adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary for their purpose. The [regulation's data principles](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj) provide context for that decision. Set access and retention rules for audit material before collection starts.

## How should reviewers handle missing or disputed evidence?

Use distinct outcomes for a supported pass, a supported gap and an unresolved case. A missing record may mean a step was missed, or it may mean the step happened without a record. Do not turn that uncertainty into a definite breach.

Ask the employee and the work owner for context before closing a case. They may have used an approved exception, followed a team instruction or relied on a policy version the reviewer did not have. Record their explanation and check it against the available evidence.

Keep a short case note with the test, source of evidence, reviewer decision and reason. If the evidence is incomplete, state what is missing and whether it can still be obtained. Give the person concerned a way to correct factual errors in the note.

An unresolved case can still reveal a process problem. If review happens but leaves no trace, the fix may be a lightweight record at the point of release. If the rule itself is unclear, ask its owner to settle the wording before the next audit.

## How do you review tool use outside the approved list?

Compare the tool named in each selected work record with the list that applied at the time. Check for exceptions, pilot approvals and changes in the tool's permitted use. A tool approved for drafting public text may have a different status for work involving customer information.

If a tool is absent from the list, ask how it entered the workflow and what information was sent. Find out whether a team lacked access to an approved option or received unclear instructions. Those facts shape the fix and may reveal a wider access problem.

Do not assume that a missing central record proves the work never received approval. Ask the relevant manager and tool owner for the decision, then assess whether it covered the work. If no reliable record exists, report the uncertainty and identify who can resolve it.

Use the [AI register](/glossary/ai-register) and its related records to check ownership and approval status. Where a tool needs review, pause the affected use if the risk warrants it and route the case to the person who can decide.

## How can you audit without collecting too much employee data?

Set a clear purpose for each evidence request and name the people who need access. Tell employees what will be reviewed and how they can raise a concern. The [worker monitoring guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/employment/monitoring-workers/) is a useful prompt for planning that review with the right advisers.

Prefer a record of the relevant decision over a broad collection of employee activity. For example, ask for proof that a reviewer approved a customer reply before asking for the full message history. If the narrow record leaves the test unresolved, document why more detail is needed.

Store sensitive excerpts apart from the report and give access only to the review team. Agree when the material will be deleted or reviewed for continued retention. Check whether legal, employment or internal rules require a different approach for the case.

A finding sent to a manager should contain enough detail to support a fair response. A report for a wider group can describe the control gap without naming the employee or reproducing the content. Keep the link between the summary and the restricted case note for follow-up.

## What should the audit report say?

Report the scope, work period, policy versions, sources and selection method before giving results. Show how many selected cases could be tested and how many remain unresolved. If you report a rate, state its denominator and avoid presenting it as a rate for the whole workforce.

Group findings by cause as well as by rule. A missed review may stem from a work design that has no clear approval point. Unapproved tool use may reflect a policy gap, an access gap or a decision by an employee. The evidence should support whichever explanation the report gives.

Describe both the effect of a finding and the limit of what you know. A record may show that restricted material was sent to a tool without showing what happened to it afterward. Refer that question to the right security or privacy owner rather than filling the gap with a guess.

The [risk management framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) can help frame findings around risks and controls. For a board audience, use the site's [AI risk report guidance](/blog/what-should-a-board-request-in-a-quarterly-ai-risk-report) to select decisions that need attention.

## How do you close findings and check the fixes?

Give each finding an owner, an agreed action and a date for review. The owner should be able to change the process, not merely remind staff of the rule. Record any decision to accept a risk and the person who made it.

Match the fix to the cause. An unclear rule may need a revised example, while a missing approval step may need a change to the workflow. A staff member may need targeted instruction when the rule and the available tools were clear.

Ask for evidence that the fix exists, then test whether it works on later cases. A published policy change shows that wording changed, but it does not show that employees can follow the new process. If the check fails, reopen the finding and agree on a different action.

Keep the case record and the fix record linked without distributing sensitive material. The [governance starter kit](/resources/ai-governance-starter-kit) can support the owner and action records. Close the finding when the reviewer has seen enough evidence to support that decision.
