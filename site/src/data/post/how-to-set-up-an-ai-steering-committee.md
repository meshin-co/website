## How to set up an AI steering committee: what should its remit cover?

Start with a short charter that names the decisions the group can make. A useful remit covers shared priorities, funding choices, unresolved risks, and exceptions that cross team boundaries. It should also name the work that stays with delivery teams. The sponsor signs the charter and makes it available to people who submit proposals.

Write the authority in terms a requester can test. For example, a business owner may approve a tool within an agreed budget and risk boundary. A proposal that shares customer data across departments may need the committee to settle ownership and conditions. A change outside the committee's delegated authority goes to the sponsor or board.

The charter should name a chair, a record keeper, and a route for disagreements. It should say how members make a decision when someone is absent. It should also say who can pause work while a serious concern is reviewed. Check the remit against your [AI operating model](/glossary/ai-operating-model), so the forum fits the roles already in place.

## Who needs a seat at the table?

Choose members for the decisions they can make, not just their job titles. The business representative should be able to speak for the teams that will use or fund the work. A technology representative should understand delivery, support, and the effect on existing systems. Security and data representatives should be able to explain the limits their teams need applied.

A senior sponsor gives the group a path when members cannot agree. The chair owns the agenda and makes sure each proposal has a decision to request. A record keeper maintains the decisions and follows up with owners. One person may hold more than one role if the company is small.

Invite legal, procurement, finance, or staff representatives when their input is needed. For a customer-facing change, include someone who can describe how customers may be affected. Ask each standing member to name a deputy who can make decisions in their absence. A deputy without authority can attend, but the chair should record any decision that still needs approval.

Keep the group small enough for a real discussion. If the same specialist attends every meeting, consider whether that person needs a standing seat. If a member never has a decision or action, ask whether they need the papers instead.

## Which decisions should come to the committee?

Send a proposal to the committee when it needs a trade-off that no single owner can settle. Examples include competing requests for a shared budget, a tool used by several departments, or a risk that one team would impose on another. A project owner should bring a recommendation rather than an open request for general feedback.

| Decision                         | Usual owner               | When to bring it to the committee                                 |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Routine use within agreed limits | Business or service owner | The use crosses an agreed boundary                                |
| Shared tool or budget            | Named sponsor             | Teams disagree on funding or ownership                            |
| Exception to an internal rule    | Owner of the rule         | The exception affects several teams or lacks an agreed approver   |
| Serious incident response        | Incident lead             | A later decision needs funding, policy change, or senior approval |

The committee can approve a proposal, approve it with conditions, defer it for missing evidence, or reject it. Each outcome needs a named owner and a record of the reason. A conditional approval should say what must happen before use starts and who checks that it happened.

Agree the boundaries with existing budget holders and risk owners before the first meeting. The committee should not assume authority that belongs to someone else. For help sorting requests before they reach the agenda, see [how to prioritize competing AI projects](/blog/how-to-prioritize-competing-ai-project-requests).

## What should a decision paper contain?

Give requesters a small template that asks for the decision in the first line. The paper should name the proposed use, the people affected, the owner, and the choices available. It should also show the expected cost, the data involved, and any open concerns. If a cost or risk is unknown, say who will find it out and when.

Ask the requester to describe what happens if the group does nothing. That option helps members compare a proposal with the work already under way. A request for a shared tool should say which team will pay for it and who will support it. A request for an exception should name the rule owner and the date when the exception will be reviewed.

The [risk management resources](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) can help the chair frame questions about ownership and risk. Use them to check the paper's prompts against the work the group actually sees. The [AI governance starter kit](/resources/ai-governance-starter-kit) is another place to look for working materials.

Send papers before the meeting, with enough time for members to consult their teams. If the key facts are missing, return the paper rather than filling the agenda with guesses. The chair can still accept an urgent request and record why the usual preparation was shortened.

## How should meetings produce decisions?

Set a meeting rhythm that matches the volume and urgency of requests. At each meeting, begin with overdue actions and changes to previously approved work. Then take decision papers in the order set by the chair. Leave routine project updates in written reports unless they need a choice from the group.

For each item, the requester states the decision and the chair asks members to name any objection. The record should capture the outcome, reasons, conditions, owner, and review date. It should also name the people who took part and note any unresolved objection. A person who leaves with an action should know what result to bring back.

Agree a minimum set of decision-makers for each type of choice. If a required person is absent, defer the item or use the agreed delegate. Do not treat silence from an absent owner as approval. When members cannot agree, the chair records the options and sends the issue to the named sponsor.

Store the record where the teams responsible for delivery can find it. Limit access where the papers contain sensitive data, but avoid a private log that project owners cannot use. Link decisions about a specific tool to its entry in the [AI register](/glossary/ai-register).

## What happens between meetings when an issue is urgent?

Give staff an urgent contact route that works without waiting for the next agenda. The incident lead should be able to contain a problem under the existing response process. That may mean stopping access, pausing a workflow, or preserving records for review. The chair and sponsor need to know who can make each of those calls.

Write down the trigger for notifying the committee or sponsor. For example, a data concern that affects several teams may need a senior choice about whether work can resume. The incident lead should send the facts known so far, the action already taken, and the next decision needed. Mark unknown facts as unknown rather than waiting for a complete account.

The committee may meet outside its normal rhythm if a decision needs several owners. If members cannot gather, the charter should name an authorised delegate and a way to record the call. The delegate should tell affected owners what was decided and bring the record to the next meeting.

Keep response work with the people trained and authorised to carry it out. Use the committee to resolve the trade-offs or funding questions that remain. The article on [AI incident roles and escalation](/blog/how-to-assign-roles-and-escalation-paths-for-ai-incidents) covers how to assign that response path.

## How does the committee work with executives and the board?

Agree the boundary between management decisions and board decisions before a difficult proposal arrives. The committee can prepare a recommendation, but it should not claim an approval reserved for the board. The sponsor should name the route for issues that exceed the committee's budget, risk authority, or remit.

A board paper should explain the decision sought, the options considered, and the consequences of delay. Include the owner who will carry out the decision and the evidence that will come back. If the committee could not agree, state the disagreement rather than presenting a false consensus. The board can then ask for the missing facts or make the choice within its own authority.

For routine oversight, give executives a concise view of open decisions, material risks, and actions past their due dates. Link each item to the fuller record rather than copying sensitive detail into every report. Report a closed action only after its owner has shown what changed.

The guidance on [which AI decisions need board approval](/blog/which-ai-decisions-need-board-approval) can help with that boundary. The [responsible AI principles](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles) also offer prompts for discussing who may be affected by a decision.

## How can you tell whether the committee is working?

Review the decision log with the sponsor after the group has handled a useful range of requests. Look for decisions that sat waiting for an owner, conditions that nobody checked, and issues repeatedly deferred for the same missing facts. Ask requesters whether they knew where to send a proposal and what the group needed from them.

Compare the charter with the decisions members actually made. If routine requests fill the agenda, delegate them within clear limits. If the group never sees cross-team risks until after launch, ask project owners where those risks should enter the process. Change the intake form or the member list only after identifying the gap.

Check whether approved work has a named owner after staff changes. A decision may still stand while its owner, budget, or assumptions have changed. Ask the new owner to confirm the record and raise any condition the team can no longer meet.

For a broader view of how roles and records fit together, consult the [management system guidance](https://www.iso.org/standard/81230.html). Bring proposed changes to the charter back to the sponsor for approval, then tell requesters which route to use next.
