## What belongs in a one-page board memo for AI strategy?

The board memo AI strategy one page format puts a decision, its evidence and its limits in front of directors. It should be short enough to scan, but complete enough to support a vote or a request for changes. Start with the choice management wants the board to make. Then explain the business need, the proposed scope and the conditions attached to it.

The memo also needs an owner with the authority to act. A group can contribute to the work, but the board should know who will answer for delivery and report setbacks. Put the spending request beside the decision, including any limit management is asking the board to approve.

Reserve space for the most consequential risks and the next review point. Supporting papers can hold the details without making directors search for the basic case. The [AI readiness and risk assessment](/services/ai-readiness-and-risk-assessment) is a route to a board memo when management needs a clearer view of its starting position.

## What decision should the board be asked to make?

Choose a decision that matches the evidence available. If the business has a defined problem but little test evidence, ask for permission to investigate within a spending limit. If a test has produced credible results, ask for a specific next step and explain what must remain under review.

Avoid a broad request to approve AI across the company. That wording leaves open which work may start, who can commit funds and when the board will see results. A narrower request gives management room to act while preserving a clear point for further approval.

The following example is illustrative. Its wording is a model for a decision, not a claim about an actual company or project.

| Memo element | Illustrative wording                                                       |
| ------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Decision     | Approve a bounded test of an internal support workflow.                    |
| Purpose      | Find out whether staff can resolve routine requests with less manual work. |
| Limit        | Keep the test within the approved budget and named team.                   |
| Condition    | Review quality, data access and staff feedback before any wider use.       |
| Owner        | Assign an accountable business executive to report the result.             |

If the board wants a different limit or a stronger review condition, record that change with the decision. The owner can then plan against an agreed mandate rather than infer one from the discussion.

## How should the memo make the business case?

Describe the problem in terms the board can judge without knowing the tools. Name the work affected, who does it today and why management wants to change it. A useful case explains the cost of the current approach or the service problem it creates. It also says what evidence would show that the proposed work is worth continuing.

Choose a measure that follows the work through to an outcome. For a support workflow, speed alone may hide poor answers or extra checking by staff. Pair any speed measure with a way to judge quality and the effort required to correct mistakes. Record the starting point before the test if management wants to make a fair comparison.

Keep estimates separate from observed results. If staff have identified a possible saving but have not tested it, say so. Explain the main assumption behind the estimate and who will check it. The [readiness scorecard](/resources/ai-readiness-scorecard) can help structure the questions management asks before committing to a direction.

## Which risks deserve space on the page?

Select risks that could change the board's decision, the scope of the work or the conditions for release. These may include access to sensitive information, unreliable output, a weak route for staff to correct errors, or a supplier dependency that is hard to replace. The right list depends on the proposed use, not on a generic ranking.

For each material risk, state the response and the person responsible for it. A promise to manage risk gives directors little to assess. A stronger statement says what access will be restricted, who will review the result and what happens if the review fails. Put the full record in a supporting paper, where reviewers can see the basis for each judgement.

The [US risk management guidance](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) offers a way to structure that wider review. For work involving personal data, the [UK data protection regulator's guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/artificial-intelligence/guidance-on-ai-and-data-protection/) is a useful source for questions about data use. The memo should identify the relevant concern and point to the assessment rather than claim that a short review settles every legal question.

## How much spending should management request?

Ask for the amount needed to reach the next decision, not an open commitment to an eventual rollout. Include the expected cost of staff time, supplier fees, review work and any changes needed to existing systems. Where the amount is uncertain, show the estimate as a range in the supporting paper and say what could move it.

The board needs to know which costs are included in the request and which would require another approval. A test may appear inexpensive if it excludes the work needed to connect data, check results or train staff. Make those boundaries explicit even when another team will carry the cost.

Set a limit that the accountable owner can monitor. Describe what happens if the work is likely to exceed it, including who may approve a change and when the matter returns to the board. For a choice between building and buying, the [cost comparison guide](/blog/build-vs-buy-ai-3-year-tco) offers a way to examine costs beyond the initial purchase.

## Who owns delivery and when does the board hear back?

Name a business owner for the outcome and give that person a clear route to the teams doing the work. The owner should be able to explain progress, risks and spending in the same terms used in the memo. Technical, security and legal staff may each own parts of the review, but the board still needs a single point of accountability.

Agree on a return point tied to a decision rather than a routine status report. For example, management might return when the test results are ready and ask whether to stop, revise the scope or fund a wider release. The report should include the evidence behind the recommendation and any material change in risk or cost.

Make the stop conditions visible before work starts. These might include an inability to protect the data used in the test, results that do not meet the agreed quality bar, or a forecast that exceeds the spending limit. The [operating model guide](/blog/how-to-choose-an-ai-operating-model-for-a-mid-size-company) can help management assign work across teams without obscuring the board's named owner.

## What evidence should sit behind the memo?

A short memo works best when directors can inspect the records behind its claims. Keep a record of the proposed use, the people affected, the data involved and the result of any tests. Link the memo to the relevant documents and identify who maintains them. If a record does not yet exist, say what management will produce before the next decision.

Separate evidence from judgement in those papers. A test result can show what happened under the conditions tested. Management must still judge whether those conditions reflect the work the business intends to do. Note gaps in coverage and explain how the next review will address them.

The [AI system register](/glossary/ai-register) can provide a place to track systems and their owners. The [OECD AI principles](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles) can also inform questions about transparency and accountability. Use those questions to test the proposal, then keep the memo focused on the choices directors must make.

## How should the memo handle uncertainty and dissent?

Put a material disagreement in the memo rather than waiting for it to surface in the meeting. If the business owner wants a wider test and the security team recommends a narrower one, state the difference and the reason for management's proposal. Directors can then decide whether the proposed conditions are sufficient for the next step.

Use plain labels for what is known, estimated and still to be checked. Avoid giving a forecast the visual weight of a measured result. If the decision rests on an assumption about staff adoption or supplier performance, name it and explain how the team will test it. The board can approve a bounded test while keeping that assumption open.

Before sending the paper, ask someone outside the project team to read the decision and spending request without an oral briefing. If that reader cannot tell what approval would permit, revise the wording. Send the memo with its supporting papers and record any conditions the board adds to its approval.
