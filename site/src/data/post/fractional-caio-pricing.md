## How does fractional CAIO pricing work at Meshin?

Meshin uses a fixed structure for each offer rather than an hourly rate. The amount reaches you in writing before work starts. The [pricing page](/pricing) is the starting point for the offer structure, and the [charging FAQ](/faq/how-do-you-charge-for-the-work) covers how the work is charged.

A fixed structure still needs a clear scope. The written proposal should say what work is covered, who takes part and what the buyer will receive. It should also explain any conditions that could change the agreed work. Without those details, a fixed fee can leave the buyer unsure what has actually been bought.

Before accepting a proposal, ask the person doing the work to walk through it. Take a current decision or task and ask whether it falls inside the agreement. If the answer depends on access, input from your staff or a supplier decision, have that dependency recorded. This makes it easier to spot gaps before they become a dispute about scope.

The amount is only one part of the commitment. Time from your own staff, access to systems and outside supplier charges can all affect the cost of getting work done. Ask for those commitments to be visible alongside the fee.

## Which type of work should the fee cover?

Start with the result your team needs, then choose the shape of the engagement. A review of the current position has a different end point from getting a workflow ready for use. Ongoing leadership calls for another kind of agreement, with a way to set priorities as needs change.

| Work needed        | Question to settle before agreeing a fee                       |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Assessment         | What will be reviewed, and what will the team receive?         |
| Delivery work      | Which workflow is in scope, and who can approve its use?       |
| Ongoing leadership | Which decisions and meetings need continuing attention?        |
| Handover           | Who takes over each task, and what material will they receive? |

The [readiness and risk assessment](/services/ai-readiness-and-risk-assessment), [production sprint](/services/90-day-production-sprint) and [fractional CAIO retainer](/services/fractional-caio-retainer) describe different ways to arrange the work. Read the relevant offer before asking for a proposal.

A buyer can also use the [readiness scorecard](/resources/ai-readiness-scorecard) to identify gaps worth discussing. The scorecard can frame the conversation, but it cannot settle the scope on its own. Name the business problem, the people affected and the decision you need to make. Those details give a proposed engagement a boundary that both sides can test.

## What should a written proposal include?

A proposal should connect the fee to work that a buyer can recognise. Ask for a description of the outcome, the work products and the people who must take part. A useful proposal also names the person who can accept the work for the client.

For delivery work, ask how the team will decide whether a workflow is ready for use. The answer might include a test set, a review by the people who use the result and a clear route for recording defects. Ask who prepares the test material and who decides whether an unresolved issue blocks use.

For ongoing work, ask how priorities will be set and revisited. The agreement should distinguish time spent advising from decisions that remain with your company. It should also explain how the buyer raises a new request when existing work is already in progress.

A work product can be a decision record, a plan or a piece of working software. The name alone is not enough to judge its value. Ask what it will contain, who will use it and whether your team can maintain it. Where the answer depends on access to an existing system, confirm who can grant that access.

## Which costs should I ask about separately?

The proposal should say whether the fee includes outside services or whether your company pays them directly. Ask about model access, hosting, software subscriptions and any specialist work brought in for the engagement. The answer may differ by project, so record it rather than relying on an assumption.

Where a supplier charges for use, ask which account will hold the service and who can see the bills. A spending limit can help, but someone must also decide what happens when the limit is reached. Agree whether work pauses, switches to a different service or needs a fresh approval.

Cloud costs deserve the same care as the leadership fee. The [AWS cost guidance](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/cost-optimization-pillar/welcome.html) gives buyers a framework for thinking about cloud spending. Use it to prepare questions about who monitors use and who has authority to change a service.

Your team's time is another commitment to make visible. An internal engineer may need to grant access, review changes or own the system after handover. A legal or security colleague may need to review a supplier. Ask the proposed lead to identify these needs before your team commits to a schedule.

## Who makes decisions during the engagement?

Price is hard to compare if proposals assign different amounts of decision work to the buyer. Ask which decisions the fractional lead can make and which need approval from your company. Name a person for each approval, including a substitute if that person is away.

For example, the lead may recommend a supplier while the buyer keeps the authority to sign its contract. A delivery team may prepare a workflow while a business owner decides whether its output is fit for use. Record those boundaries in the proposal or in a work plan linked to it.

Also agree how to handle a decision that cannot wait for the next planned meeting. Name the route for raising it, the person who responds and what the team should do while waiting. This matters when a supplier changes access or a test finds a serious defect.

The [AI operating model page](/glossary/ai-operating-model) provides a starting point for discussing how responsibilities fit together. Use the proposal to apply those responsibilities to the people who will actually do the work. A title alone does not establish who can approve a purchase, accept risk or stop a release.

## How should we agree changes to the work?

Set a process for changes before the first request arrives. A new workflow, an extra supplier review or a change in access can alter the work. Ask who can request a change and who must approve it for each side.

The change record should describe the request, its effect on the current plan and any proposed change to the fee. It should also say what happens to work already agreed. The buyer can then choose to accept the change, defer it or keep the current scope.

Do not rely on a meeting note that leaves the fee open. Ask for the revised terms in writing before the extra work begins. If the change needs a quick decision, agree how the written record will be confirmed and stored.

It is useful to distinguish a defect in an agreed work product from a new request. If a report omits something the proposal promised, the parties should address that under the existing agreement. If the buyer wants a new analysis, discuss it as a scope change. The proposal should explain how questions about that boundary will be resolved.

## How do I compare proposals from different providers?

Give each provider the same description of the problem and ask them to state their assumptions. Compare the work they propose, not just the amount at the bottom. A lower fee may cover less delivery work or leave more review and coordination with your staff.

Look at the proposed access to the person doing the work. Ask who attends key meetings, who reviews work products and who stands in if that person is unavailable. The answer helps you judge whether the proposed arrangement fits the decisions your company needs to make.

For work involving a live service, ask how the provider will discuss reliability and service expectations. The [Google Site Reliability Engineering book](https://sre.google/sre-book/service-level-objectives/) explains how service objectives can focus that discussion. Your agreement should say who chooses the measures and who acts when the service falls short.

Check the proposed approach to company data as well. The [FTC guide to protecting personal information](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/protecting-personal-information-guide-business) gives businesses questions to consider about handling data. Ask each provider what access they need, where information will be stored and how access will be removed. These answers can expose work that a fee comparison alone misses.

## What should happen when the engagement ends?

Agree the end terms while both sides are planning the work. Ask how notice works, what happens to work in progress and whether any charges remain after the end date. The [retainer exit FAQ](/faq/how-does-the-retainer-end) is a starting point for that conversation.

Name the internal owner who will receive each active task. That person needs access to the work products, open decisions and supplier contacts, not just a final meeting. Ask for a list of unresolved issues with their next action and owner.

Where software or data is involved, settle access and ownership in the written agreement. Ask which accounts belong to your company, who holds the code and how any temporary access will be removed. The [ownership FAQ](/faq/who-owns-the-code-and-the-data) addresses code and data, while the [handover playbook](/resources/handover-playbook) can help your team plan the transfer.

A handover needs an acceptance check. Have the receiving owner find a document, open the relevant system and explain the next decision in their own words. If they cannot do that, record what is missing and agree who will supply it. The work should remain usable after the outside lead leaves.
