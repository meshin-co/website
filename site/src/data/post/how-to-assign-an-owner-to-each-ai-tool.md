## Where should you start with the list of tools?

To decide how to assign an owner to each AI tool, start with its actual use. Pull together the tools staff buy, build or access through a shared service. Ask department leads to check the list against the work their teams do. The [AI system register](/blog/how-to-build-an-ai-system-register-in-a-week) gives you a place to record the result.

A product name alone may hide several uses. A writing tool used for internal notes presents a different set of decisions from the same tool used in customer support. Record the business purpose, users, data involved and where the output goes. Note any links to other systems or actions the tool can take.

Do not wait for a perfect inventory before assigning people. Give known uses a proposed owner and mark gaps for review. If staff identify a tool with no clear sponsor, ask the leader of the team using it to decide who will take the next step. Record that interim contact without calling the assignment complete.

The [risk management framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) offers context for assigning responsibilities across a tool's life. Use it to prompt a discussion about who can act when the use changes.

## Who should be the named owner?

Choose someone close enough to the work to understand the consequences of a poor output or an unwanted action. The owner should know who uses the tool and why. They should also be able to reach the people who control its budget, access and technical setup.

A department manager or product lead may fit when the tool serves a clear business process. A platform lead may fit when the service is shared across the company. The buyer or contract holder can be a useful contact, but check whether that person can make decisions about actual use.

Ask the nominee directly whether they accept the work. Describe the expected reviews, the support available and the decisions they can make. A manager who assigns the role should check the person's workload and make room for it. Record the acceptance and the date it takes effect in the register.

If a nominee lacks authority, agree on a route to the person who has it. If no one can take the role, leave the assignment open. The responsible business leader should decide whether to restrict or pause the use while the gap is resolved.

## What should the owner be able to decide?

Set the boundary of the role before asking the owner to sign up. The owner needs enough authority to keep the recorded use accurate, request access changes and raise a concern. They also need to know which decisions require another person's approval.

| Decision or task                   | Person to name         | Record to keep                     |
| ---------------------------------- | ---------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Business purpose and permitted use | Tool owner             | Scope and approval contact         |
| User access and technical settings | Administrator          | Access process and control contact |
| Contract and supplier questions    | Contract contact       | Terms location and renewal contact |
| Risk or incident escalation        | Owner and risk contact | Route, backup and response record  |

This split is a starting point. Adapt it to the people who can actually do the work. For example, an owner may request that access be removed while an administrator makes the change. The record should say who can approve a pause and who can carry it out.

Agree on what happens when people disagree. A business leader might resolve a dispute about whether a use should continue, while a security lead advises on the technical exposure. See the [incident roles and escalation guide](/blog/how-to-assign-roles-and-escalation-paths-for-ai-incidents) for the response path. The tool owner should know that path before a problem occurs.

## How do you assign ownership for a shared tool?

A shared tool can have a platform owner without putting every business decision on that person's desk. The platform owner can coordinate the supplier, shared settings and access process. Each department should name a contact for its own use when the purpose, users or data differ.

Write down where the platform owner's remit ends. For example, the platform owner might control which features are available, while a department contact checks whether staff use them for an approved purpose. Name the person who can settle conflicts between those decisions.

Keep the shared service and its distinct uses linked in the register. That link lets an owner find affected contacts when a feature changes or access needs review. It also helps a department contact reach the person who can change a platform setting.

For a tool supplied by a vendor, name an internal owner even when the vendor runs the service. That owner should know where the contract sits and whom to contact about changes. The [vendor assessment questionnaire](/blog/ai-vendor-assessment-questionnaire-template) can help the owner find open supplier questions. For data handling, use the [privacy guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/artificial-intelligence/guidance-on-ai-and-data-protection/) as a prompt when setting the boundaries of each use.

## What belongs in the ownership record?

Keep the record short enough that the owner can maintain it. Put it beside the tool's purpose and users, rather than in a separate list that may drift out of date. The [governance starter kit](/resources/ai-governance-starter-kit) can support the wider recordkeeping work.

Include the owner's name or work contact, their team and the date they accepted. Add a named backup and the person who can approve changes beyond the owner's remit. Record links to the contract, access process and relevant risk decisions. Make sure those links work for the people who need them.

Describe duties in terms someone can check. The owner might confirm the business purpose, review the user list with an administrator and report changes to the team that keeps the register. State what evidence they should leave, such as a dated review note or a decision record.

Avoid putting passwords, sensitive prompts or personal data into the ownership field. Link to the approved location for those materials instead. Check that the backup can find the record and reach the right contacts when the owner is away.

## How do you check that an assignment works?

Test the assignment with a real decision rather than relying on the job title. Ask the owner how they would find the user list, request an access change and report a concern. Then ask the administrator and backup whether they know what they would do.

If the answers do not match, fix the handoff. The owner may need access to a report, a contact for the supplier or approval to ask an administrator for help. Record the agreed change and the person responsible for completing it. A name in a register does not give someone those powers by itself.

Review the assignment when the tool gains a new use, a team changes or the supplier alters an important feature. The owner should check whether the existing scope still fits and bring unresolved changes to the right approver. The [access review guide](/blog/how-to-run-a-quarterly-ai-tool-access-review) sets out a separate process for checking who can use a tool.

For changes to a system your team runs, the [secure development guidelines](https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/resources/guidelines-secure-ai-system-development) provide prompts for technical staff. The owner should know who will assess a change and where the decision will be recorded.

## What happens when an owner leaves?

Make the handover part of the assignment, not a task discovered after departure. The outgoing owner should identify open decisions, known issues and upcoming supplier or access work. Ask the backup and the incoming owner to review that list together.

Transfer the record as well as the title. The incoming owner needs the current purpose, contacts, approval limits and links to supporting material. Check that they can open the records and reach the people who administer the tool. The [handover playbook](/resources/handover-playbook) offers a place to plan that transfer.

A manager should appoint an interim contact if the successor has not yet accepted. Give that contact a clear remit and a route to the manager for decisions beyond it. Keep the ownership gap visible until the successor has the time and authority to take it on.

When a tool is being withdrawn, the owner should also coordinate its closure. Confirm who removes access, handles retained data and checks any contract commitments. The [tool retirement guide](/blog/how-to-retire-an-ai-tool-when-its-vendor-contract-ends) covers those steps. Close the ownership record only after the people doing that work have confirmed their tasks are complete.
