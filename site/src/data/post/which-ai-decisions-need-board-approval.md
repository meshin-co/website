## How do you decide what belongs with the board?

To decide which AI decisions need board approval, start with the company's reserved matters and delegated limits. Read the existing rules for contracts, budgets, investments, and risk acceptance. Then ask whether the proposed use falls inside them. Legal and governance staff should check the governing documents and applicable law before the board adopts a new approval schedule.

The question concerns the decision, not the presence of a model. Buying a tool, using it with customer records, and changing a product are separate decisions. They may have different owners and may reach the board at different points. A contract within the budget can still carry data terms that exceed management's authority to accept risk.

Make the boundary visible in a short schedule. Name the decision, the person who can approve it, and the condition that sends it higher. Link each condition to an existing policy or a specific board resolution. Where a limit is missing, ask the board to set one rather than leaving staff to guess.

The [board memo template](/blog/board-memo-ai-strategy-one-page) can help turn that schedule into a decision request. Keep the schedule with the company's other delegation records, where both directors and managers can find it.

## Which AI risks should reach the board?

Bring a proposal to the board when it asks the company to accept risk beyond management's agreed authority. That may include a use with serious effects on customers or staff, a sensitive data use, or a failure that could disrupt a critical service. The board should set the boundary for its own business rather than rely on a generic risk label.

A decision paper should describe who may be harmed and how the harm could occur. It should also state what checks exist, what could still go wrong, and who can stop the use. For example, a tool that drafts staff guidance has a different impact from a tool used to decide who receives a service. The same vendor name does not make the decisions equivalent.

If management cannot show that a proposed use fits the agreed limit, pause the approval route until someone with authority resolves the gap. A committee can review the case, but its recommendation is distinct from approval unless it has delegated power to decide.

Use the [risk ranking approach](/blog/how-to-rank-ai-use-cases-by-risk) to compare proposals before they reach directors. For a broader set of prompts about human impact, consult the [OECD principles for AI](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles).

## When does spending need a board decision?

Apply the same spending authority used for other business commitments. Count the proposed contract alongside the changes needed to put it to work. A low initial fee may sit beside costs for integration, staff time, support, and exit. The proposal should show which costs are estimates and which are firm commitments.

Check whether the business is asking for a new budget or reallocating an existing one. The approval route may differ even when the total cost looks similar. If several teams plan to use the same tool, assign an owner for the full commitment. A set of separate requests should not conceal a decision that exceeds a delegated limit.

Directors should also see what happens if the vendor changes its terms or the company leaves. Include any known notice period, expected migration work, and limits on retrieving data. Where a cost remains unknown, state who will resolve it before signing.

The [build-versus-buy cost guide](/blog/build-vs-buy-ai-3-year-tco) can help management frame the alternatives. The board can then compare the requested commitment with the value and risks of waiting.

## When should data use go to the board?

Send a data decision upward when the proposed use exceeds an approved policy or delegated risk limit. The request should identify the data involved, the intended purpose, and the people or vendors with access. It should also explain whether the data will leave the company's systems and what controls apply.

A board decision may be needed even when the tool itself has already been approved. Using a tool for public material does not establish permission to send it employee records. Likewise, a vendor contract cannot settle every later question about a new use. The owner should check the actual data and purpose before the work begins.

Ask the privacy and security owners to state their advice in the paper. If they disagree, directors need to see the disagreement and the options for resolving it. The [employee data decision guide](/blog/how-to-decide-which-employee-data-can-be-used-in-ai-tools) helps frame that review. The [data protection guidance for AI](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/artificial-intelligence/guidance-on-ai-and-data-protection/) offers further questions for the people assessing the proposal.

## Can management approve a pilot and return for launch?

Yes, if the pilot stays within management's authority and its approved scope. The pilot request should name the users, data, purpose, and place where results will be used. It should also describe what the team will test before asking for a wider release. A trial with real customers may need different authority from an internal test.

Separate permission to learn from permission to make an enduring change. Management might test draft answers with staff while reserving a customer-facing launch for a later decision. The launch paper can then show actual test results, remaining defects, and the proposed support plan. If the test exposes an unapproved use, the owner should stop that use and seek the right decision.

Set a clear end to the pilot or a review point for continuing it. Decide who removes access, handles retained data, and pays any costs after the trial. The [AI register guide](/blog/how-to-build-an-ai-system-register-in-a-week) can help the owner keep the pilot's scope and status visible.

## What should the board see before it votes?

A useful paper asks for a specific decision and explains the consequences. It names the accountable executive, the proposed use, and the limit that sends the request to directors. It also distinguishes a request for approval from an update for information. Directors should not have to infer which choice management wants them to make.

| Part of the request | What directors need to see                                               |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Scope               | Users, purpose, data, and the business change proposed                   |
| Alternatives        | A viable comparison, including delay or retaining the current process    |
| Cost                | The full expected commitment, uncertainties, and available budget        |
| Risk                | Potential harm, controls, unresolved issues, and the proposed risk owner |
| Decision            | The action requested, conditions, and authority needed to proceed        |

Evidence should fit the decision rather than fill a standard pack. A vendor presentation may describe a product, but management must explain the intended use. Test results should include material failures and the limits of the test. The [risk management framework resource](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) offers a basis for structuring that discussion.

## What conditions should attach to approval?

The board can approve a proposal subject to conditions that management can verify. Conditions might limit the users, data, spending, or business process covered. They can also require a named owner to resolve an identified issue before launch. Write each condition so the owner can tell when it has been met.

Avoid approving a broad ambition when the paper describes only a narrow use. If directors approve a customer support trial, the record should say which customer interactions it covers. A later plan to make eligibility decisions should follow its own approval route. This keeps an early decision from becoming permission for an unrelated use.

The minutes should record the decision, any dissent, and the conditions imposed. Put the operational detail in the approval record or linked paper, where staff can use it. The owner should confirm completion of conditions to the person or group named in that record. An unresolved condition should remain visible rather than disappearing into a project plan.

## What changes should send a project back?

A project should return for a decision when a change exceeds its approved scope or authority. Examples include a new customer group, a different use of sensitive data, or spending above the approved limit. A proposed change in how much discretion the tool has may also require a fresh review. The trigger depends on what the original approval actually covered.

Record return triggers when the board makes its decision, not after a dispute arises. Name who checks changes and who receives the request. If the change stays within management's delegated limits, the owner can document that judgment and proceed through the normal route. If authority is unclear, seek a decision before expanding the use.

Vendor changes deserve the same scope check as changes made by the company. Ask whether an update affects the approved purpose, data handling, or risk controls. The [vendor update review](/blog/how-to-review-ai-vendor-model-updates-after-deployment) describes a way to inspect those changes before relying on them.

## Who can act before the board meets?

An urgent incident needs an immediate operational owner with authority to contain harm. That person may need to pause a tool, restrict access, or move work to a manual process. The response plan should say who can take those steps and whom they must notify. It should also identify decisions that still require board approval.

Containment and acceptance of ongoing risk are separate choices. An executive may have authority to stop a service without having authority to accept a new long-term risk. If restarting would breach a reserved limit, the owner should seek the required approval. The request should explain the incident, the remaining exposure, and the consequences of continued suspension.

Agree on a contact route for directors when waiting for a scheduled meeting would be harmful. Check that any urgent decision procedure fits the company's governing documents. The [incident escalation guide](/blog/how-to-assign-roles-and-escalation-paths-for-ai-incidents) can help management assign the operational roles before an incident occurs.

## Can a committee decide instead of the full board?

A committee can decide only within the authority the company has given it. Its charter and the board's delegation should make that authority clear. A committee may be able to review a proposal, challenge the evidence, and recommend a decision. That work alone does not transfer a reserved decision away from the full board.

Where several committees have a stake, name the group that coordinates the review. Security, customer impact, and spending may all matter to the same proposal. The coordinating group should put unresolved differences in the paper rather than send directors conflicting recommendations. Management still needs one accountable owner for carrying out the decision.

The [committee oversight guide](/blog/how-should-boards-divide-ai-oversight-among-committees) covers how to divide that review work. To put the approval boundary into practice, ask the company secretary and executive owners to map a current proposal against the existing delegation. Bring any gaps to the board for an explicit decision.
