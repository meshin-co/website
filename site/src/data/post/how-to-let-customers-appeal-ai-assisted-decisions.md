## How to let customers appeal AI-assisted decisions?

The practical test of how to let customers appeal AI-assisted decisions is whether a reviewer can change the outcome. Start with a notice that identifies the decision and points to a review channel. Customers should find that channel without knowing which software contributed to the outcome.

Choose a path that fits the service, such as a reply, web form or phone line. Offer another route for customers who cannot use the main channel. Support staff should record an appeal made during an ordinary conversation without sending the customer back to the start.

Give each appeal a case record and a named owner. That owner tracks the case through the answer and any required correction. The [AI governance starter kit](/resources/ai-governance-starter-kit) can help a team organize the wider roles and records.

## What should the decision notice say?

Name the outcome in terms the customer will recognize and connect it to the relevant application or account. Explain the main reasons the business can support, rather than presenting a score as the reason by itself.

Tell the customer how to request review and what happens after the request arrives. Include the expected response time, any applicable deadline and a contact method for access needs. If the team misses its target, send an update without waiting for the customer to chase the case.

Describe the role software played in that particular decision. A system may make a recommendation for staff to consider, or a decision may proceed without staff review. Check the actual workflow before describing either case. The article on [disclosing AI use to customers](/blog/when-should-a-business-disclose-ai-use-to-customers) addresses that notice more broadly.

Protect another person's sensitive details and avoid exposing checks that would make fraud controls easy to defeat. Those limits still leave room to explain the outcome and its review path.

## How does a customer submit an appeal?

Ask for the decision reference, a contact method and the reason for the challenge. Allow customers to say they do not know why they received the outcome. That answer should prompt a request for clarification, not an automatic rejection.

Let people attach relevant evidence without demanding documents before the team knows they are needed. A customer disputing an address may need a different route from someone challenging a missed payment. Explain which documents may help and how to send them securely.

Confirm receipt through the customer's chosen contact method where possible. Include the case reference, the next step and a way to add information. If identity checks are needed, match them to the information and changes the review will involve.

Give frontline staff a short route to flag urgent cases. A disputed restriction may prevent a customer from using an essential service during review. The receiving agent needs a clear way to seek a pause or faster review.

## What evidence does the reviewer need?

Keep a record of the outcome, the inputs used and the policy applied at the time. Record the system's recommendation separately from the final decision and any staff reasons for accepting or changing it. This lets the reviewer see which part of the process the customer disputes.

Preserve enough context to identify the system version and the source of key data. If a supplier provides a result, agree on the case information it will supply. Give staff a route to request that information when a case arrives.

| Record                                 | Use during review                              |
| -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Original outcome and reasons           | Check what the customer is challenging.        |
| Relevant inputs and their sources      | Find data that is missing, stale or disputed.  |
| System recommendation and staff action | See how each contributed to the outcome.       |
| Customer evidence and later changes    | Assess the challenge and trace any correction. |

Show the reviewer the customer's account and new documents alongside the original evidence. Mark information that is missing, stale or disputed. Keep the original record distinct from corrected data so staff can explain what happened.

Limit access to staff who need the information and set a retention rule for appeal records. The article on [AI chat retention](/blog/how-to-set-ai-chat-retention-rules) offers a related approach to access and deletion. Apply those choices to attachments and supplier correspondence as well as the case record.

## Who can change the outcome?

Assign review to someone with enough distance from the original decision to consider the challenge fairly. The reviewer needs authority to uphold, change or reverse the outcome. Where specialist approval is needed, name the approver and provide a direct route to them.

Show the underlying evidence without making the system's recommendation the only prominent item. Ask the reviewer to record the reason for the outcome and how they handled conflicting evidence. A button that merely confirms the original result gives little assurance of a fresh review.

Give reviewers options when evidence is incomplete or a supplier cannot provide a record. They may ask the customer a focused question, seek another source or use an approved fallback process. If the business cannot reconstruct the system's reasoning, the reviewer should not invent one.

The wider design of [human oversight](/glossary/human-oversight) affects what staff can see and do. Test the appeal reviewer's authority against a real case: they must be able to order a correction and see it carried out.

## When should an appeal pause a decision?

Set the pause rule before staff face an urgent case. Identify outcomes that could cause serious or immediate harm, then name who can suspend them. A pause might stop an account closure or hold a collection action, depending on the service and available authority.

A pause can have costs for the customer or the business, so the reviewer needs to assess both. Record the reason for the decision, the person responsible and when to check it again. Tell the customer what has been paused and what remains in effect.

Legal duties depend on the decision and jurisdiction. For cases covered by Article 22(2)(a) or (c), the GDPR requires safeguards including human intervention and the ability to contest the decision. Read the [official regulation](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng) and ask counsel how it applies to the workflow.

Do not assume that involving a person settles the legal question. Counsel needs to understand what the system does, what staff decide and how the outcome affects the customer. The operating process should reflect that assessment rather than a label on a diagram.

## How should the team close the appeal?

Send the customer the review outcome in plain language and explain the main reasons. Address the evidence they supplied and say what will happen next. If the original decision stands, describe any further review route and how to use it.

When the outcome changes, assign the correction to a team with access to the affected system. A changed case note alone may leave the original restriction or charge in place. Check each downstream action that matters, such as restoring access, correcting a balance or notifying a supplier.

Close the case only after the responsible team confirms the change. If a correction cannot happen immediately, tell the customer what remains and when to expect an update. Give support staff enough context to answer follow-up questions without making the customer repeat the whole story.

Where an appeal reveals incorrect data, decide whether the source record also needs correction. Otherwise the next decision may draw on the same error. Keep the appeal history separate from the current record so staff can understand the original outcome and the fix.

## How can a business check that the process works?

Before launch, walk a sample case from the notice through to the corrected outcome. Include a customer who cannot use the main form and a case where supplier evidence is missing. Ask staff to show how they would pause an urgent action and who would approve it.

Review a sample of closed cases for missed steps, weak reasons and corrections that did not reach the live system. Look for repeated disputes about the same data source or rule. Send findings to the original workflow's owner, with a date for checking the fix.

Track signals such as time to acknowledge, time to resolve and cases reopened after closure. Read those measures alongside the case details and the actions taken. A fast response can still leave the wrong restriction in place, while a longer case may need more evidence.

The [risk management framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) provides a wider setting for reviewing AI risks. The [guidance on explaining decisions made with AI](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/artificial-intelligence/explaining-decisions-made-with-artificial-intelligence/) can help teams examine what customers need to understand. Use findings to change the notice, review path or original workflow, then test the revised process.
