## When does a customer need to know?

Tell a customer before an AI interaction begins or an AI-supported outcome matters to them. The need is strongest when the tool speaks to the customer, handles their information, or shapes a result they may rely on. A notice should arrive while the customer can still make a meaningful choice.

Start with the customer's experience rather than the name of the model. Ask what the person sees, what information they provide, and what happens next. A customer may reasonably expect a support reply to come from a person, for example, even when a tool drafted it. That gap between the experience and the process is a reason to consider a clear notice.

The stakes matter too. A tool that suggests article titles for staff review presents a different issue from one that influences access to a service. For the latter, explain the tool's role and the route for correcting an error. Check legal and contractual duties in either case.

## Which uses need a prominent notice?

Place the notice where the use becomes relevant, not only in a general policy. A label beside a chat box can tell a customer what they are about to interact with. A notice on a form can explain what happens to the information submitted there. The wording should be visible before the customer takes the action.

| Customer experience                                     | Notice to consider                                                                             |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A customer starts a chat with an automated system       | Identify the system at the start and explain any route to a person.                            |
| A customer submits material for an AI-supported service | Explain the tool's role and link to the relevant data notice.                                  |
| A tool helps produce a consequential outcome            | Explain its role, the review that actually occurs, and how to challenge an error.              |
| Staff use a drafting aid for routine internal work      | Check data handling and customer promises before deciding whether a separate notice is needed. |

These are starting points for a review, not rules that settle every case. The same tool can call for different wording in different workflows. A person who checks spelling in a draft has a different role from a person who approves a claim in a customer reply.

## What should the notice say?

Use plain words for the action the tool performs. Say whether it answers questions, drafts a reply, sorts a request, or helps staff review information. Describe the customer's next step and the limits that matter for that step. Avoid technical detail that hides the practical point.

A chat notice might say: “You are chatting with an AI system. You can ask for a person if you need help.” Use the second sentence only when that route exists and staff can follow through. If a transfer has limits, put those limits near the promise.

For a staff-reviewed reply, wording might say: “Our team uses AI to draft some replies. A team member reviews this reply before it is sent.” Confirm that the review covers the reply in question. If staff merely sample replies, say that instead of claiming each one is checked.

Keep data details consistent with the privacy notice and the vendor's actual settings. For product claims, compare the draft with the [FTC's advice on AI claims](https://www.google.com/search?q=%22Keep+your+AI+claims+in+check%22+Federal+Trade+Commission). Ask support staff to test whether they can explain the wording without adding promises.

## What do legal and contract checks cover?

Under the EU AI Act, providers must ensure people interacting directly with certain AI systems are informed, unless that is obvious in context. Read the [law's transparency provisions](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj) alongside the product design. The relevant role, system, exceptions, and applicable dates need a legal check before a team relies on a conclusion.

The GDPR requires certain privacy notices to mention automated decision-making and profiling covered by Article 22. Read the [data protection regulation](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj) for the conditions and detail that apply. An AI notice does not by itself settle the separate question of a lawful basis for processing personal data.

Review customer contracts as well as public wording. A contract may restrict where data goes, name approved suppliers, or require notice before a material service change. Sales and procurement teams should check the actual agreement rather than assume that the website covers it.

Have counsel assess the jurisdictions and facts involved. Record the result in terms the product team can use: what must appear, where it must appear, and what triggers another review.

## Does internal AI use need customer disclosure?

An internal tool does not always need its own customer-facing label. The decision depends on what the tool sees, what it produces, and whether its use changes a promise made to customers. A staff member using a tool to brainstorm a generic meeting agenda may have no customer-facing use to disclose.

The answer can change when staff put customer information into that same tool. Check whether the data may be sent to the vendor, retained, or used for purposes beyond the task. Compare those facts with the privacy notice and customer agreements. The [customer data FAQ](/faq/are-models-trained-on-our-customer-data) offers a starting point for the vendor questions.

Also check claims about how work is performed. If a contract promises named-person review, an automated step cannot quietly stand in for it. If the service description promises an entirely human process, the owner should assess that wording before introducing a drafting tool.

Document a decision to give no separate notice. Include the workflow, the data involved, and the reason the customer experience remains unchanged. That record makes the next review easier when the tool gains a new use.

## When should an existing customer hear about a change?

Tell existing customers before a change takes effect when the new workflow alters their service, data handling, or a significant result. Check the contract for any notice method or lead time. A release note alone may not reach the person who agreed to the original terms.

Describe what changes and what stays the same. For example, a support team might add a tool that drafts replies for staff. The notice should say whether staff still review each reply and whether customer messages go to another provider. Avoid calling the change optional unless the customer can actually decline it.

Give account teams a way to answer questions before the launch. They need the approved wording, the date or event that starts the change, and a contact for contract issues. If customers have choices, explain how to exercise them and what each choice means for the service.

Where the change does not affect the customer experience, keep the internal decision record. Revisit it if the tool later handles different data or takes on a larger role.

## How should a business explain review and correction?

Describe human review by its actual scope, not by a broad label. A person might approve every output, check a sample, or only handle cases sent to them. Those processes give customers different expectations. The notice should match the process in use at the point of contact.

Test the route a customer would use to report an error. A working route needs a clear contact, a team able to investigate, and a way to return an answer. Do not promise an immediate human response if the service cannot provide one. Explain any limits in language the customer can act on.

For support replies, keep a record of who can correct an answer and how corrections reach the customer. The article on [reviewing AI-generated customer support replies](/blog/how-to-review-ai-generated-customer-support-replies) covers the review workflow. Align the notice with that workflow before the tool goes live.

If the process changes from full review to sampling, update the wording before the change. The same applies when a tool starts sending replies without staff approval.

## Who owns the notice after launch?

Give the customer journey an owner who can approve both the notice and the process behind it. Product, support, privacy, legal, and vendor teams may each hold part of the answer. One person should be responsible for bringing those pieces together and resolving gaps.

Keep the decision beside the system record. The [AI register](/glossary/ai-register) can link the use case to its owner, data flows, customer notice, and review date. Record the version of the wording and where customers see it. Include the evidence for claims about human review and vendor data use.

Set change triggers rather than waiting for a complaint. A new vendor, a model update that changes behavior, a new data source, or a shift in review scope can make old wording wrong. The owner should check whether each change calls for a revised notice or a fresh legal review.

The [vendor assessment questionnaire](/blog/ai-vendor-assessment-questionnaire-template) can help the team collect answers before buying a tool. Keep those answers current when the service or vendor terms change.

## What if a notice was missed or was wrong?

Pause the affected claim while the owner checks what customers were told and what the system actually did. Preserve the relevant wording and records of the workflow. Find out which customers encountered the mismatch and whether it affected their data, choices, or outcomes.

Correct the notice at the point of use. If customers relied on an inaccurate statement, assess whether they need a direct message and a chance to correct a result. Legal and contract teams should decide what further notification is required. Do not assume that changing a web page resolves a past error.

Give support staff a short account of the issue and an escalation contact. They should be able to distinguish a confirmed fact from an open question. Where the service offered a human handoff that did not work, restore the route or remove the promise while the team fixes it.

Close the review by assigning the fix to a named role. Add a check to the release process so the next workflow change prompts a review of customer wording.
