## How can boards verify AI cost savings against a baseline?

Start with a written claim that names the work, period and business unit. Ask the team to show what that work cost before the AI system was introduced. The comparison should cover the same types of tasks and the same cost categories. Where the work changed, the team should explain the difference.

A baseline may come from payroll records, supplier invoices or service reports. Each source has limits. Payroll can show staff costs, for example, but it may not show how much time people spent on the task. A manager's estimate can fill a gap, provided the board can see that it is an estimate.

Workload matters as much as the total bill. A lower spend alongside fewer completed cases may say little about the effect of the tool. Ask for cost per completed case as well as total cost, with the same quality threshold in both periods.

The [Green Book](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-green-book-appraisal-and-evaluation-in-central-government) offers a broader approach to comparing options and assessing their effects. For a board review, the point is to make the comparison clear enough to challenge.

## What would have happened without the AI system?

The before period is a starting point, not always a fair comparison on its own. Work may have grown, demand may have fallen, or a supplier may have changed its price. Any of those changes can affect costs without being caused by the AI system.

Ask the team to describe the most credible alternative: how the work would have been done without the tool. That might be the former process at the new volume of work. It might instead be a planned contract change or a revised staffing plan that was already approved.

The choice needs evidence. Finance can review budgets and contracts, while the business owner can explain changes in demand. If a comparable team still uses the former process, its records may help. The teams still need enough in common for the comparison to be useful.

When the alternative is uncertain, show a range of estimates rather than a single precise figure. Record the assumptions that cause the range to change. Directors can then judge whether the decision holds even if the saving is smaller than expected.

## Which savings have reached the accounts?

A claim becomes more credible when finance can trace it to a reduced payment or an agreed budget change. A cancelled supplier service, a lower invoice or fewer paid overtime hours can provide that trail. The team should show the relevant records and confirm that the cost has not moved to another budget.

Staff time needs a separate treatment. If people finish work faster but remain on the same pay, the organization has gained capacity. That may be valuable, but it has not necessarily reduced cash spending. The board can ask what staff did with the released time and whether that work met an agreed need.

Avoided spending also deserves its own line. A team may expect the tool to prevent a future hire or contract increase. Ask for the approved plan and the reason the spend is no longer needed. Until the decision is reflected in the budget or a contract, label the amount as an estimate.

| Claim                 | Evidence for the board               | Treatment                                |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------- |
| Lower supplier spend  | Contract change and invoices         | Cash saving when paid costs fall         |
| Staff time released   | Work records and a check of time use | Capacity unless spending falls           |
| Planned spend avoided | Approved plan and revised budget     | Estimate until the decision takes effect |

## Which costs should the saving include?

Ask for a cost view that follows the work from purchase through daily use. Vendor charges are a starting point. The team should also include setup, staff training, support and any extra infrastructure needed to run the system.

Human review belongs in the calculation too. Staff may need to check outputs, fix errors or handle cases the tool cannot finish. Those costs can sit in a different team from the one that bought the software. The board should ask both teams to agree on where the time and expense appear.

Separate costs paid once from costs that recur. That helps directors see whether the result reflects a lasting change or a period with unusual setup spending. Include contract commitments and known exit costs when deciding whether to extend the work. Do not count the same shared cost against every project without explaining the allocation.

The [GAO cost estimating guide](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-195g) sets out practices for developing and managing cost estimates. The [build-versus-buy kit](/resources/build-vs-buy-ai-kit) can help teams lay out the costs and assumptions for a decision.

## How can directors tell whether quality held up?

A lower bill is not enough if the work creates more corrections, delays or complaints. The team should agree what counts as a completed case before comparing costs. It should also use the same quality checks for work done with and without the tool.

Ask for a sample of finished cases and the results of review by people who understand the work. The sample should include routine cases and cases that are hard to handle. If reviewers find an error, the report should show the time and cost of fixing it.

Look for work that has shifted out of sight. A support team may receive more follow-up calls, or staff may spend time repairing data after the main task appears complete. Those effects should appear in the cost review even when another department pays for them.

The board need not inspect each case. It does need to know who checked the work, what they examined and whether any gaps could change the result. If the quality evidence is weak, hold the savings claim open until the team can test it.

## How should the board check the underlying data?

Ask the claim owner to trace the reported result back to records another person can inspect. That trail might run from a board paper to a finance report, then to invoices or payroll entries. For work volume, it may run to a case log or service report.

The [GAO guide to assessing data reliability](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-283g) is a useful reference for reviewing data used as evidence. A practical check starts by asking who entered the data, what it covers and whether records are missing or duplicated.

Access to the tool is not proof that it completed useful work. Usage records can show whether staff opened or used a service, but they need to be matched to completed cases and quality checks. The [adoption telemetry article](/blog/adoption-telemetry-measuring-if-ai-is-actually-used) covers ways to examine use without treating activity as the outcome.

Where estimates replace records, ask the owner to mark them clearly. Finance can then test the inputs that matter most to the decision. A claim with an unfilled gap may still support a pilot decision, but it should not be presented as a verified cash result.

## Who should sign off on the claim?

The business owner should explain the work and the change in demand. Finance should check the cost treatment and reconcile cash claims to the relevant records. The team that checks quality should report any defects or delays that could affect the result.

Ask those owners to resolve differences before the figure goes into the board pack. If they cannot agree, the pack should show the competing views and the missing evidence. A single figure without that context can hide a dispute about what was counted.

The board can request an independent check when the claim supports a material spending decision or when the records are weak. The reviewer needs access to the source data and enough distance from the team that prepared the claim. The scope of that check should be stated in the paper.

A concise [board memo](/blog/board-memo-ai-strategy-one-page) can carry the result, its limits and the decision requested. Keep the detailed calculation available for directors who want to follow the trail.

## When should the board revisit the result?

Review the claim after a change in price, workload or the way staff use the tool. A saving shown during a trial may change when the service handles more work or needs extra support. The owner should tell the board which assumptions are most sensitive to those changes.

Set a review date when approving further spend. At that point, compare the forecast with actual invoices, staffing costs and work quality. Show any difference from the earlier estimate and explain whether it comes from volume, price, errors or another cause.

A board may decide to continue despite uncertain cash savings if the system meets another clear business need. Keep that decision separate from the savings claim. Name the benefit being sought, the evidence needed and the person responsible for reporting it.

For the next board pack, ask finance and the business owner for a single reconciled claim. Include the baseline, cost trail, quality check and unresolved gaps. Directors can then approve, revise or stop the spend on evidence they can inspect.
